Process mining analyzes event logs from information systems in order to understand and enhance real processes. These processes, however, change while an event log captures them. Such concept drift may concern not only the order of activities but also the behavior of resources and the situation of all running cases together. Most existing detectors focus on control flow, while publicly available test data rarely includes drifts related to resources.

This thesis introduces an unsupervised, state-based framework for monitoring a process from three perspectives: intra-case, resource, and inter-case states. For each perspective, states are computed through feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, and clustering. Changes in the distributions of these states over time are then used as drift signals. While traditional control-flow-based detectors focus on one perspective, the proposed framework can identify drifts in all three perspectives and, through its states, provide information about what has changed. To support the evaluation of such drifts, our generator Rheon injects drifts of two drift modes and nine drift types from all three perspectives into synthetic event logs. The framework itself is implemented in the library kairo and a dashboard with an AI assistant.

We evaluate the framework on 34 logs of the Padella benchmark and on 45 Rheon logs with 103 drifts. In 29 of the Padella logs, a drift was injected into a real log. With the hand-tuned configurations, the framework achieves F1-scores ranging from 0.862 to 0.933, and with configurations created by AI using our agent skill \texttt{kairo-drift-analyst}, from 0.309 to 0.774. The difference mainly stems from precision and, on Rheon logs with several drifts of one perspective, also from recall. Many false positives of the agent configurations correspond to real changes that are unknown to the benchmark or are reactions in other perspectives. Thus, the states are useful and explainable indicators of concept drifts as long as appropriate configurations are found. The actual problem is, therefore, finding appropriate configurations, and rules of thumb for window lengths and thresholds offer a starting point for this.
